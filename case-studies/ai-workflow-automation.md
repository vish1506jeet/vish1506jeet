# AI-Powered Workflow Automation

**Company:** upGrad
**My role:** Product Owner — owned this from the first conversation to the board presentation

## The problem, in plain terms

Four teams — Sales Operations, Finance, Content, and Customer Success — were all spending a big chunk of their week on work that didn't really need a human doing it. Someone copying data from one system into another. Someone reformatting the same report every Monday. Someone manually passing a file from one team to the next because there was no other way for it to get there.

Everyone kind of knew this was happening. Nobody had actually sat down and measured it. And that's the problem with "everyone kind of knows" — it doesn't get you budget, it doesn't get you engineering time, and it definitely doesn't get you in front of the CEO. So before I asked for anything, I went and found out exactly how big this actually was.

## What I did

I didn't start with a tool or a vendor. I started by going and sitting with each of the four team leads and actually mapping their work — every recurring task, how long it took, how often it happened, and honestly how boring and repeatable it was. That's where the real number came from: 40% of the weekly capacity across those teams was going into things that didn't need a person's judgment to do.

Once I had that number, the job changed from "convince people this is a problem" to "figure out how to fix it without breaking anything." A few things I made sure to get right:

- **I didn't try to automate everything at once.** I picked the 5 highest-volume, lowest-risk workflows and ran the pilot with just 2 of the 4 teams first. If something went wrong, I wanted it to go wrong small.
- **I sat with engineering early, not after the fact.** We worked through what data needed to move where, what generative AI could realistically handle versus what still needed a person to check it, and what should happen automatically if the system got something wrong. I didn't want a black box nobody trusted.
- **I wrote the acceptance criteria myself and tested against real work**, not made-up examples. If it couldn't handle an actual messy report from last Tuesday, it wasn't ready.
- **I built the business case on real numbers, not a pitch.** Hours saved, what it would cost to build, what the return actually looked like over time. I took that straight to the CEO, and the sign-off for a company-wide rollout came off the strength of those numbers, not a slide deck.
- **I treated rollout as part of the job, not a formality after the "real" work was done.** I trained all four teams myself, wrote the guides, ran live sessions, and kept tracking adoption week by week until people were actually using it day to day, not just aware it existed.

## What it actually achieved

- 40% cut in manual effort across all four teams
- 100+ people onboarded and using it
- Full adoption within 2 months of go-live
- Zero critical issues after launch
- It went in front of the board as an example of how the company should think about AI going forward, and a second phase got approved to extend it to 3 more business functions

## The decision I'd point to if you asked me what actually mattered here

Quantifying the problem before writing a single requirement. Before that number existed, this was a "nice to have" that nobody was going to fund. The moment it existed, it became a board-level conversation. That's the difference between a good idea and a funded project — someone has to do the unglamorous work of proving the size of the problem first.

The second thing I'd flag is treating adoption as part of the build, not an afterthought bolted on at the end. The rollout took almost as long as the technical work itself, and it's the actual reason this got used rather than quietly ignored six weeks after launch, which is what happens to a lot of internal tools that skip that part.
